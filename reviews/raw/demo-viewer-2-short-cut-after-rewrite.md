## Part A (frames only, written before the subtitle file was opened; unchanged)

1. What is this project? A single-page website holding 15 structured write-ups of failed AI-safety experiments, each summarised from a LessWrong or Alignment Forum post, with filters, a search box and trust labels on every card. The same entries can be searched from a command line so an AI agent can read them and write a "what has been tried" answer; every entry I saw was drafted by an AI model and is marked unreviewed.

2. Who is it for and what would they use it for? AI-safety researchers (and AI agents working for them) who are about to run an experiment and want to know whether it, or something close, was already tried and where it went wrong. The scene I was shown is someone checking a plan to steer a model's "evaluation awareness" direction to stop sandbagging. A second group is people or agents who add entries.

3. What problem, and why does it matter? The video says failed AI-safety experiments usually stay in a file drawer or in a post nobody can find, and that people repeat known mistakes or ignore evidence "one search away". It says this once, in a caption over the title card, with no example of a mistake being repeated and no statement of what the repetition costs; I had to infer why it matters. It also does not reconcile "file drawer" (never published) with the fact that its own entries come from posts that are already public.

4. How does it work? Parts I saw: (a) a web page with a left sidebar of filter checkboxes (badges, failure type, confidence, retry welcome, result direction, benchmarks, checks done, models), a search box, and a count ("15 of 15 entries"); (b) entry cards with badges (Agent-written, Unreviewed, Retry welcome), a yellow "model draft" banner, area/method/model tags, result, failure type, confidence it is a true negative, evidence, review, written-by, a quote with a link to the source post, and an "All fields" expander (hypothesis, claim_scope, limitations, revival_conditions, retry_welcome, source, contributor); (c) a command-line tool that keyword-searches the entries and prints a ranked list; (d) an "ask" step in which an AI agent writes a markdown answer with sections "Already tried", "Where it went wrong", "What to avoid", "Not found", citing entry IDs; (e) a "contribute" step in which an agent drafts an entry from a post and it lands in git as an entry file with contributor/review stamps; (f) a validator that rejects an entry with a category outside a fixed list or a quote not found verbatim in the source, and passes the 15 real ones.

5. Who or what puts content in, and who reads it? In: an AI agent (claude-sonnet-5-5, via a "contribute skill") wrote every entry visible; a human maintainer is supposed to verify later; the first five are said to become the narrator's own entries. I saw no entry written by a person. The original authors of the LessWrong posts are the cited sources. Out: a person reading the web page; an AI agent reading through the command-line search / an "ask skill" / a "plugin".

6. What was actually shown working? Shown: ticking two filters changed the count from 15 to "4 of 15"; typing "stegan" gave "2 of 15" with two matching cards; opening one card expanded to all fields; the command-line search returned three ranked results out of 8 matches; a finished answer file was displayed (not generated on screen); a git log and the contributor/review fields of one merged entry; the validator refusing two altered copies and passing 15 files. Claimed but not shown: an agent actually running the ask or contribute steps, any human contributing, any human review, filtering by date or source, the plugin, authors being asked.

7. Trust, and was it convincing? The video's answer: every card shows who wrote it, evidence level, review status, confidence it is a true negative, the exact scope tested and limitations; the validator refuses invented categories and quotes the post never said; the sample answer ends by saying everything rests on one unreviewed entry. Partly convincing on honesty (the labels are visible and the video undersells rather than oversells). Not convincing as grounds to rely on the content: all 15 entries are agent-written by the same model, 14 of 15 are unreviewed and the one reviewed entry is never shown or explained, the validator was only shown catching a bad category and a missing quote (not a wrong summary or a wrong result direction), and the basis for "confidence true negative: low/medium" is not explained.

8. Stated limits or weaknesses: entries are model drafts not yet human-verified; the sample answer rests on a single unreviewed entry and says it only scanned titles of the other 14 (read as "I did not open them"); the wiki holds only failures, so a positive result would not appear; only 15 entries; local proof of concept, not public; authors not yet asked; only LessWrong and the Alignment Forum so far; of 49 genuine failure write-ups only 9 stated enough to fill every field.

9. Tone toward the people whose work appears: respectful and non-blaming in words ("Entries describe experiments, never people"; "never judge people"; a failure "warns you about a pitfall without telling you the idea is dead"; authors will be asked and can opt out). In tension with that: every card prints the authors' full names, and the authors have not been asked yet.

10. The single thing it most wants me to remember: closest candidate is that a failed experiment should be recorded with exactly what was tested and how far to trust it, so one thin failure report is not read as "this never works" (repeated on the principles slide, in the entry scenes, and on the finding slide). I am not sure the video commits to it as the headline: the title card leads with "a wiki ... people and AI agents both write to, and both read", and the slogan "Each experiment can be less wrong than the one before it" sits in the smallest grey text on the last slide. So there is a candidate, but it competes with others.

11. Why does it start where it starts? "It starts with LessWrong and the Alignment Forum, a community that already posts candid write-ups of what did not work." That is the only reason given. It is asserted, not shown, and nothing says why not other sources or whether these posts are representative.

## Part C: questions still open after watching

1. Has anyone other than the maker and an agent written an entry? What would a human contribution look like?
2. What are "the scored ones", how were they scored, and what were the scores?
3. Which entry is the one reviewed entry (Agent-written 15, Unreviewed 14), who reviewed it, and to what standard?
4. How exactly is an agent prevented from marking its own entry reviewed?
5. Did an agent actually run the ask skill through a plugin against this wiki? What did the run look like, and how long did it take?
6. How were the 212 candidate posts chosen? What does "enough to fill every required field" mean, and how often does the small model's "genuine" label err?
7. Beyond a verbatim quote, how accurate are the agent-drafted summaries, claim scope and result direction against the posts? Any measured error rate?
8. Who assigns "confidence it is a true negative", and on what basis?
9. Where do later successful retries or contradicting results go, given the wiki "holds failed experiments, so a positive result would not appear"?
10. What is the "1 stated exception" among the sources of the 15 entries, and do several entries from one post count as independent evidence (the answer rests on "one author's LessWrong post")?
11. Has any author been contacted yet, and how does an opt-out remove an entry?
12. Is there evidence that anyone has wasted compute on a dead end this would have caught?
13. Where can a viewer try it or read the code, given "not public yet"? The video gives no link or handle.
